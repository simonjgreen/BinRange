# BinRange: consolidated project story and blog notes

_Prepared 3 October 2026 from the owner's pasted early UWB discussion, the BinRange discussions, the repository, and documented field findings. This is source material for a blog post, not a claim that every remaining field test has passed._

## The short version

The starting question was simple: the collection calendar knows which bins are due, but it cannot tell whether anyone has actually moved them. Years ago, an attempt to answer that with cheap Bluetooth tags proved frustrating: batteries drained, proximity readings jittered, and the outdoor Home Assistant Bluetooth proxy was unreliable in that installation. Reading about UWB reopened the idea. BinRange then grew from a pair of USB-powered UWB development boards into a working six-bin field prototype. Each bin now carries a battery-powered radio tag; a fixed, powered anchor measures its distance, sends readings to Home Assistant through MQTT, and lets the collection calendar drive reminders. The tags report more often when moved and sleep between occasional stationary check-ins. The anchor can also deliver signed firmware updates to already paired tags over Bluetooth.

The project then acquired two things a bench demonstration could not supply: physical packaging and real collection-day evidence. A sequence of printed case failures led to a screwless overlapping-shell enclosure. An observed garden-bin collection showed that movement, departure, return and tipping could be detected, while radio coverage at the kerb and the calendar connection could still prevent the notifications from firing. Those failures prompted a repaired calendar and a more realistic remembered-**Out** state. A separate review replaced frequent tipping-automation polling with an event-driven timer. The installed system is useful and in service, but battery lifetime, outdoor coverage and unattended maintenance need longer observation.

## What problem we were trying to solve

The practical problem is a mismatch between **schedule** and **physical state**. A calendar can say “General Waste tomorrow”; it cannot answer “is the General Waste bin still by the house?” Nor does a lack of radio contact automatically mean the bin has moved. The intended outcome became a household view that shows the bins' status, calls attention to bins still Home before collection, and reminds us to bring them back afterward. Later, the desired “they have just been emptied” message added a different physical question: can the system recognise a tip rather than merely a move?

The agreed fleet is six physical bins: Garden, Recycling 1, Recycling 2, Compost, General Waste and Glass. Calendar categories do not map one-to-one to hardware: “Recycling” covers both recycling bins, and the “Food” collection maps to Compost. This mapping is a useful detail for the blog because it shows why calendar names alone could not serve as device identities.

## Before BinRange: why UWB, and what was rejected

The original BLE version tried to infer whether a bin was nearby from cheap tags and an outdoor Home Assistant BLE proxy. The owner reported three problems: fast battery consumption, inconsistent/jittery readings, and proxy reliability at the time it was built. The UWB discussion reframed the measurement from received signal strength to radio time of flight. The aim was a robust **home zone**, not a map of the garden or the centimetre-level tracking of runners that inspired the research question.

The first research answer actually preferred a wired reed or Hall sensor with a passive magnet **if** each bin had a fixed berth. The owner ruled that out immediately: the bins do not dock in consistent or precise places. That constraint, together with the desire to distinguish individual bins, made a radio range measurement worth testing. One anchor near storage was the proposed starting point; a second was only a contingency if the storage and kerb positions had similar distances or the link was obstructed. Three-anchor triangulation was unnecessary for the household question.

There was a separate conversation about conventional UWB positioning: three well-spaced anchors are the geometric minimum for 2D, four for 3D, and clustering anchors along one wall makes a position solution less stable. The owner then said this was **just curiosity**, not a change of requirements. BinRange continued as a one-anchor home/out zone detector. This exchange is useful context because it rules out telling the story as an unfinished attempt at full location tracking.

The owner also set a physical requirement early: the bin-side hardware had to resemble a small self-powered puck, not an Arduino-sized exposed board. The initial research spec imagined a custom DWM3001C tag with a CR123A cell, then revised toward a CR2450 coin cell and a small moulded enclosure after considering existing UWB pucks. Commercial tags were attractive **only if** their firmware or ranging protocol could be used with a self-built anchor; AirTag-like size did not imply open interoperability. Kinesis and Sunway were explored as candidates, and two Qorvo DWM3001CDK development kits were suggested for a proof of concept before selecting final tag hardware. The actual experiment used Makerfabs WROVER/DW3000 boards, and the deployed bins use KKM K4W tags with removable CR2477 cells.

Two **27 August 2026 orders** make the move from research to hardware concrete. A Makerfabs receipt records **two [ESP32 UWB DW3000 WROVER boards](https://www.makerfabs.com/esp32-uwb-dw3000.html)** for US$91.60 plus US$32.04 shipping, US$123.64 total. Later that day, the [KKM K4W packaged-tag](https://www.alibaba.com/product-detail/Ultra-Wideband-UWB-Tags-for-RTLS_1601366460275.html) supplier said its small enclosed tag had an accelerometer, could run the owner's firmware, and had a programming connection accessible via headers; it offered a matching programming jig. The supplier quoted **ten sample tags at US$25 each, a US$35 jig and US$45 shipping, totalling US$330**. Its order record was marked completed and listed a shipping term of 15 days after initial payment, but it does not establish the actual dispatch or delivery date. The supplier described the enclosure as IP67 and offered a schematic after sample payment; those were supplier statements, not independently verified weather or documentation results. The development boards and packaged tags were therefore sourced in parallel, before the September ranging experiment. These records explain why both kinds of hardware were on hand, though not why Makerfabs was preferred over the originally suggested Qorvo development kits. The prices here are historical order figures, not current quotes.

One shared shopping link needs careful labelling: RobotShop's [“Makerfabs ESP32 UWB Pro”](https://uk.robotshop.com/products/esp32-uwb-pro) describes a **DW1000** board. It is a related product reference, **not** the DW3000 model identified by the Makerfabs receipt and used in BinRange. Likewise, the K4W listing's multi-year battery figure is stated for a BLE advertising interval, not for this project's UWB firmware, so it is not evidence of BinRange battery life.

Some early numbers were **design targets and projections**, not outcomes: a 30-minute heartbeat, a greater-than-two-year battery ambition, rapid movement detection and very few false reminders. The field prototype later used its own settings and still lacks the current measurements and long observation needed for a battery-life claim. The original “no recent packet means Unknown, never Out” rule was also refined by field evidence: silence alone still proves nothing, but a previously observed departure can be remembered as presumed Out.

## How the build unfolded

### 1. Prove the radio link first — 7–8 September

The earliest accessible BinRange working session began with **two Makerfabs ESP32-WROVER/DW3000 boards connected by USB** and the request to see whether they could range over UWB. We added a local web view so the boards could be separated and observed without carrying a cable between them, then measured known distances and tried an outdoor walk.

This stage established both the promise and the limits of the technology. In the recorded two-board tests, an upright orientation improved success at about 10 m from 37% to 100%; a longer, slower radio configuration also worked where the initial short/fast setting did not. A calibrated measurement at a surveyed 10.1 m averaged 10.0912 m with a 0.030 m standard deviation in that setup. The outdoor development-board walk was reliable to roughly 30 m, intermittent beyond that, with the furthest recorded reading at 37.28 m. A car could completely block the path. These are **development-board observations**, not guarantees for a tag mounted under a bin rim.

The software direction changed during the same discussions. We considered an ESPHome component, then dropped it because it added little to the intended system. The anchor instead publishes MQTT and exposes separate per-tag devices and diagnostics to Home Assistant. The existing Waste Collection Schedule integration supplies dates; BinRange supplies movement, range and health. That left the measurement path independent of Home Assistant and avoided an extra BinRange server or cloud service.

At this point the second WROVER board could impersonate different tag identities for integration testing. That helped build the dashboard and MQTT discovery before real tags arrived, but those dummy devices were later cleaned out of the live Home Assistant setup.

### 2. Make it work with real, battery-powered tags — 18–20 September

The actual tags were KKM K4W units with an nRF52833, DW3110 UWB radio and LIS3DH accelerometer. Their arrival changed the work from network experiments to careful hardware bring-up. A spare Nesso device became an SWD programming probe for the clip-on pogo-pin jig. The development tag went first; packaged units were commissioned one at a time, physically labelled, associated with a bin, then returned to their cases with batteries fitted. The distinction between the vendor's printed label, immutable chip identity, chosen UWB ID and Bluetooth address mattered. A printed QR code was not treated as proof of the on-air identity.

Battery life was a design constraint from the beginning, but the discussion moved away from a fast fixed reporting rate. The chosen default behaviour was motion-triggered reporting at about **5 seconds while moving**, settling after quiet, and a **10-minute stationary check-in**, with UWB sleeping between attempts. A separate six-hour absence warning was proposed to catch a missing tag without confusing normal slow check-ins with failure. Voltage, wake counts, motion state and missed exchanges became telemetry. The project explicitly held back from turning a short voltage trace into a battery-life claim. Remote interval controls were later added and verified end to end; the installed fleet was then changed to **30-minute stationary check-ins** while retaining 5-second moving reports. The 10-minute figure remains the firmware default in the general documentation, so a blog post should distinguish default behaviour from the live setting.

The safe-update requirement became a substantial part of the project. First installation needs SWD; routine application updates were designed as signed BLE transfers through the anchor, with the previous application retained for rollback. An update is only reported successful after the tag itself shows the **exact expected image hash and local confirmation**. The tests exercised bad images, interrupted uploads, loss of power at selected stages, watchdog recovery and anchor absence. A persistent anchor identity and existing Bluetooth bonds had to survive updates. The first complete anchor-and-tag OTA journey succeeded, though one bench run still needed a remote retry. Later, the production anchor and all six tags were updated over the air without re-pairing. That proves the route works; it does not prove every future update will be unattended.

Commissioning exposed a specific multi-tag bug: the pinned Bluetooth library sized one kind of stored peer record by simultaneous connections rather than the number of permitted bonds. The first pairing of a further tag could fail even though keys had been stored. A reproducible backend test led to a capacity fix, after which a new physical peer paired without wiping existing bonds. This is a good example of why the leap from **one demo tag** to **six real devices** was more than repetition.

### 3. Let the installation dictate the enclosure — 19–26 September

The WROVER case started as a dimensioned OpenSCAD design. A screw-based version passed an initial fit check, but tiny nuts, bolts and PLA tolerances made it awkward, and the board could rattle because the fixings did not locate it positively. The project owner redirected the design toward a **zero-screw, pure-PLA case**: tapered pins to locate the board, printed flex features to hold it down, and a lid that actually stayed closed.

The iterations were physical feedback, not just CAD revisions. External flexible catches held the board but barely retained the lid. An internal lip improved the fit; deeper hooks improved retention but could still be pulled free. Enlarging those hooks to the wall depth broke both roots during insertion. A torsion-arm variant was drafted, then research into other printed snap-fit enclosures suggested a better change: overlapping base and lid walls with several shallow catches rather than two tall, vulnerable ones. The user-tested v7.0.0 was described as “really good.” v7.1.0 widened the board-locating cone roots and centred the vents; it became the accepted working case. A later v7.2.0 DIN-rail base and clip exists as an **experimental CAD candidate**, not a proven replacement for v7.1.0.

The enclosure history is worth retaining in a blog: the print failures explain the final shape. CAD collision checks could establish that parts should mate; only a print showed whether the catch survived insertion and resisted the board springs. The anchor was subsequently wall mounted in an enclosure, and tags were fitted to all six bins. The repository includes cleaned installation photos and case renders.

### 4. Turn readings into useful Home Assistant behaviour — 20–27 September

The daily dashboard was deliberately separated from diagnostics. The ordinary view should show the next collection, compact bin states and problems needing attention; the technical view retains ranges, reception and anchor data. For this site the initial location rule was **inside 10 m = Home; beyond 10 m = Out**, subject to a fresh reading. It is a local threshold, not a universal one: one anchor measures distance, not direction, so two locations at the same radius cannot be distinguished.

The notification schedule settled on four grouped phases, sent to a deliberately configured phone group:

| Time | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| 18:00 before collection | Remind about due bins still confidently Home |
| 21:00 before collection | Urgent reminder if they are still Home |
| 06:00 on collection day | Final urgent reminder if they are still Home |
| 18:00 the following day | Bring-in reminder for due bins still Out |

The automation records attempts per phase to avoid duplicate pushes and uses the Waste Collection Schedule calendar for due categories. Unknown or unavailable location is not enough evidence to accuse someone of forgetting a bin. The separate return message originally meant “scheduled bins are still out,” **not** “they were definitely collected.” A public, placeholder-based YAML example and setup guide were committed; the household's entity IDs, phone membership and credentials were kept in ignored local records. The phone screenshots in the README show the desired messages, while the installed automation and field tests determine when they are actually sent.

When the reminder schedule was designed, the owner asked whether collection-day telemetry might eventually identify emptying. A later, early-October retrospective of the 22 September collection showed why that was difficult: General Waste, both Recycling bins and Compost stopped reporting during much of the collection window and reappeared around 17:15–17:17; Garden and Glass continued reporting. Wake counters increased during some gaps, showing activity without locating its moment or cause. Without the owner's observed handling and collection times, the history could not label ordinary handling versus emptying. Meanwhile, the 27 September “just emptied” request became a new cross-device feature. Instead of trying to infer collection from time, range or generic motion alone, the tag uses its existing accelerometer to measure gravity-relative orientation from an upright reference. A sustained tilt **strictly greater than 90°** records a persistent tip event; the anchor carries it to MQTT, and Home Assistant treats a recent tip on a due, confidently Out bin as an **emptying assumption**. Multiple qualifying bins are grouped for 60 seconds. The tag and anchor releases were installed on all six bins, and the Home Assistant automation was enabled. This is a designed heuristic, not proof that a council crew emptied the bin: an owner can tip a bin too, and a tip may reach Home Assistant too late to qualify.

The first implementation checked pending tips every five seconds. The user challenged that as excessive for events that occur only a few times a week. It was replaced with an MQTT-led flow and a timer that runs only while a grouping or recovery deadline is pending. The saved ledger supports duplicate suppression and recovery after a Home Assistant restart. The ordinary scheduled reminders remain a fallback.

Making the project public required its own clean-up. The early development history contained local installation detail, so the public repository was started with a sanitised initial commit on 20 September. Reusable instructions, firmware and selected screenshots were kept; credentials, physical identifiers, household schedules and provisioned images stayed in ignored local storage. Later product photos were resized and stripped of metadata. A subsequent health review found old configuration/reboot endpoints that needed admin protection, a JSON-escaping defect and obsolete development controls; the source fixes were verified locally, while installation of those firmware fixes was reported separately from the review. Public availability should also not be read as a blanket open-source licence: the repository had not selected one at the time of writing.

### 5. A closely examined Garden collection exposed the gaps — 28 September

The Garden bin provided a useful end-to-end field observation. Recorded movement began at **07:05**, distance crossed the 10 m Home/Out threshold at **07:05:14**, and reports then stopped arriving while the bin was away. A single tip was reconstructed at about **09:27** from the event age carried in the later report. Reports resumed at **10:25**, by which time that tip was about **58 minutes old**. The bin crossed back inside the threshold at **10:25:17** and settled near its original storage distance. Normal stationary reports resumed.

Several parts worked: departure and return were visible when fresh reports arrived, the tag retained the tip through the radio gap, and settling resumed. But the tip exceeded the automation's **30-minute acceptance limit**, so the emptying message did not send. Other bins kept reporting, and the anchor showed no MQTT failure during the gap; the exact reason the Garden tag was unreachable at the collection position was not established. This cycle did **not** validate the grouping message or duplicate protection on a real collection.

The put-out reminders also did not send. Their traces showed the scheduled runs executed but found no due calendar events. Investigation found the council source configured twice, once in YAML and once through the integration UI; the empty YAML calendar blocked the working integration calendar. The duplicate was removed, the calendar/update action repaired, and the calendar again displayed upcoming events. This was an integration/configuration failure, not evidence that the position logic alone was enough.

The radio gap also changed the location model. Initially, an Out bin with stale reception became Unknown, which was technically cautious but practically misleading: once the bin had been positively seen leaving, a long absence at the kerb should still read **presumed Out**, with reception health shown separately. Across all six bins, the revised rule retains an observed Out departure through lost contact and Home Assistant restarts; only a **fresh inside-threshold reading** returns it Home. Home followed by silence remains Unknown because no departure was observed. Return reminders can use this presumed-Out state; put-out reminders still require a fresh Home reading. This live change is reflected in current local docs and examples, some of which are still uncommitted as of this note.

## Decisions that changed along the way

| Early idea or problem | What the project settled on | Why it changed |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Two WROVER boards as peers | One fixed anchor and six battery tags | The boards established the link; packaged tags answered the real household question. |
| Cheap BLE proximity tags and an outdoor proxy | UWB time-of-flight ranging | The original system consumed batteries, jittered and was unreliable in this installation. |
| Fixed berth magnet or Hall sensor | Range-based home zone | The bins have no consistent docking spots. |
| Full 2D/3D positioning | One-anchor Home/Out classification | The multi-anchor geometry question was curiosity, not a requirement. |
| CR123A/CR2450 custom puck and Qorvo dev-kit shortlist | KKM K4W CR2477 tags and Makerfabs WROVER anchor | Separate 27 August orders put two development boards and ten programmable packaged tag samples on the way at the same time; the reason for preferring Makerfabs over the suggested Qorvo kits is not documented. |
| ESPHome component | Direct MQTT discovery into Home Assistant | A separate component added little to this architecture. |
| Frequent fixed polling | Accelerometer-triggered fast reports, slow idle check-ins | Motion matters more than constant stationary ranging; battery cost remains to be measured. |
| Ranging alone could imply location | Site-specific Home/Out threshold plus independent freshness | UWB distance is neither a coordinate nor proof of continued reception. |
| Stale Out becomes Unknown | Persist an observed Out departure, with separate radio health | The closely examined Garden cycle showed prolonged loss of coverage at the kerb. |
| Movement or elapsed time might mean “collected” | Calibrated >90° tip event, due-date and Out checks | Ordinary handling and silence were too ambiguous. |
| Poll tipping automation every five seconds | MQTT events plus a deadline timer | The event rate did not justify continuous automation runs. |
| Small screws and tall snap hooks for the case | Locating cones, board springs and overlapping shallow catches | Physical prints exposed rattling, poor retention and broken hook roots. |
| USB/jig as maintenance path | Signed BLE updates through the anchor, with SWD rescue | The installed bins needed maintainability without repeated disassembly. |

## What is established, and what still needs evidence

**Established:** six tags have been commissioned and mounted, a fixed anchor is installed, tag-initiated ranging reaches MQTT/Home Assistant, the calendar-backed reminder automation and tipping automation exist, remote check-in controls work, and signed anchor/tag OTA was demonstrated on the installed fleet. The 28 September Garden collection produced a real departure, stored tip and return trace. The case v7.1.0 is the accepted printable design.

**Still to establish:** reliable coverage at every bin's storage and collection positions; several full collection cycles in which reminders and tip notifications are actually delivered; representative whole-tag current and multi-week battery behaviour; weather exposure and mounting longevity; and repeatable unattended update connections. The live 30-minute idle setting means a stationary bin may be silent by design, so monitoring needs to distinguish a planned interval from an outage. The first Garden tip was detected **but did not cause an emptying notification**. The DIN-rail clip has not been physically accepted.

## A possible blog arc

1. **Open with the older frustration:** BLE tags drained batteries and gave jittery “nearby” hints while the calendar still could not know whether a bin moved.
2. **The choice of UWB:** reject fixed dock sensors because bins never park precisely, ask for a small battery puck, and choose a single home-zone range rather than full positioning.
3. **The first bench experiment:** two development boards, a tape-measured gap, surprising antenna orientation effects and a car blocking radio.
4. **From demo to fleet:** six identified tags, a battery budget, safe commissioning and the unexpectedly difficult update path.
5. **The case that fought back:** rattling boards, weak catches, broken PLA hooks and the accepted overlapping-shell design.
6. **Making the data useful:** the six-bin dashboard and notifications tied to the household's collection schedule.
7. **Reality at the kerb:** the 28 September Garden cycle detected the tip but missed the message; an empty calendar independently stopped reminders.
8. **Where it stands:** a working field prototype that now has honest handling of stale data, with coverage, battery life and unattended updates left to prove.

## First-person moments worth keeping

- The origin: “I had an idea a number of years ago to stick cheap BLE tags on my wheelie bins...” The original system's battery, jitter and proxy problems make a stronger opening than an abstract explanation of UWB.
- The physical constraint: “passive magnet etc is out, the 'docked' location is neither consistent nor accurate” and “i can't be sticking something that resembles an arduino on there, it's too large.” Those two corrections explain both the radio decision and the small packaged tags.
- The scope correction: “nah i was just curious.” This followed the discussion of four-anchor positioning and confirms that BinRange was never meant to map each bin in 2D or 3D.
- The print feedback: the v4 board pegs and springs worked, but the lid “can move around all over the place”; later, the deep catch roots snapped during insertion. This is the human testing that led to the accepted case.
- The installation milestone: “I THINK this is all good and working now!” was said when the anchor was wall mounted and every bin tagged. The subsequent collection-day investigation provides the honest next scene.
- The automation correction: “This feels excessive, no?” was the response to a five-second polling trigger and led to the event-driven timer.
